[engaged storyteller] But speed wasn't the only pressure. Exporters were also under pressure to make the fruit look good.

Thai growers have long treated durians with turmeric to keep their color. Some traders reportedly found a cheaper, faster shortcut: Auramine O, an industrial dye made for textiles and paper. The WHO's cancer agency lists it as a possible carcinogen, and China declared it non-edible back in 2008.

Around the start of 2025, Chinese inspectors reported finding the dye in Thai durians. From January 10, every shipment needed lab reports for the dye and for cadmium, a toxic heavy metal. Large batches were turned away at the border.

For Vietnam, the timing was brutal. In the first four months of 2025, its durian exports to China reached only about 20% of plan: around 120 to 130 million dollars, down from more than 500 million a year earlier.

Thailand moved fast. It quickly got its own labs approved to run the tests, and banned exporters from artificially coloring their fruit.

The lesson was simple. When one market buys 90% of your exports, its standards become your standards.
