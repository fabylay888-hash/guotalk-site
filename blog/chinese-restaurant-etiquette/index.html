Chinese Restaurant Etiquette: What's Real, What's a Myth, and What to Say

Published August 6, 2026 · by the GuoTalk team

The short version

  • "Leave food on your plate to be polite" is obsolete. China has an anti-food-waste law and a decade-old clean-plate campaign. Boxing up leftovers is normal and encouraged.
  • Slurping is a Japanese rule that Western travel writing imported onto China. It's tolerated, not required, and it's not a compliment.
  • Tipping isn't offensive — it's just absent. Nobody's insulted, the concept simply isn't in play. Tour guides are the real exception.
  • Don't call a waitress 小姐. Old phrasebooks teach it; in mainland China it now carries a sex-work connotation. Say 服务员.
  • The chopsticks-in-rice rule is real, but it's one of about eight taboos — and you're probably breaking the others.

First, a scoping note

Most "Chinese dining etiquette" articles are describing banquet protocol — formal, hierarchical, business-dinner rules — and presenting it as though it governs lunch. It doesn't. At a noodle shop or a hotpot place with friends, nobody is running a seating chart.

We've flagged below which rules are formal-table rules and which apply everywhere.

The things that are real

Seating (formal tables)

At a round table, the anchor seat is the one facing the entrance. Either the host sits there to run the table, or the ranking guest is placed there. Sources disagree on which, and honestly both patterns exist. The practical rule for a visitor is the same either way: don't take it yourself, and wait to be seated. The seat nearest the door is the lowest-status one, and that person tends to handle pouring and door duties.

Ordering is for the table

This is the biggest mechanical difference from Western dining and it isn't in dispute: dishes are ordered for the whole table and shared, not chosen individually. A group of four orders five or six dishes and everyone eats from all of them.

Who does the ordering is less codified than guides suggest. In 2026 it's usually whoever's holding the phone, after the table calls out what they want.

The teapot lid

Offsetting the teapot lid — sliding it so it sits crooked, or propping a hinged metal lid open — signals staff to refill the hot water. 揭盖加水.

This is strongest and most reliably understood in Cantonese teahouses and dim sum halls, where it originates, and is recognised in traditional teahouses elsewhere. At a modern chain restaurant it means nothing — press the call button.

The finger tap

When someone pours your tea, tapping two bent fingers on the table thanks them without interrupting the conversation. 叩手礼.

It's a real, living custom, and its heartland is Guangdong and the Cantonese-speaking regions. A northerner may not do it or expect it. Two fingers is always correct — some sources describe a finer hierarchy (four knuckles for a senior, one fingertip for a junior), but treat that as connoisseur detail rather than a rule you can get wrong.

You'll hear a story about the Qianlong Emperor travelling in disguise. It's a good story. There's no historical documentation for it.

Toasting

Two things that matter. Hold your glass lower than the senior person's when you clink — it's a small gesture and it's noticed. And the host toasts first; a senior may toast the whole table at once, whereas juniors toast people individually.

Know the difference between 干杯 (gānbēi, "dry the cup" — a commitment to finish the glass) and 随意 (suíyì, "as you like" — sip as you please). Declining alcohol has established polite formulas: 以茶代酒 (yǐ chá dài jiǔ, "tea in place of wine") and 我开车 (wǒ kāi chē, "I'm driving").

Fighting for the bill

Real, and it has a name — 抢单. Paying demonstrates generosity and standing, so people genuinely compete for it. In a business context the inviter pays, essentially always, and a guest's counter-offer is expected to be token.

But the ritual is narrowing. Among peers and younger diners, AA制 — splitting — is now standard for informal meals, usually handled by one person paying and sending a split request in the WeChat group. Two friends at a hotpot place in 2026 will very likely just AA it.

The things that aren't (or aren't any more)

Myth: leave food on your plate to show the host provided enough

This one is genuinely obsolete in mainland China, and it isn't just a shift in fashion — there's law behind it.

The Clean Plate Campaign (光盘行动) has been running since 2013. The Anti-Food Waste Law took effect in April 2021: it requires caterers to actively remind customers not to waste food, prohibits operators from misleading diners into over-ordering, and permits restaurants to charge customers who cause obvious waste. Menus are encouraged to list portion sizes and offer smaller portions.

Etiquette guides still repeat the old rule. The official norm is now the opposite one. And 打包 (dǎbāo) — boxing up leftovers — is completely normal, not shameful.

One surviving exception worth knowing: at Lunar New Year's Eve dinner, a little fish (鱼 yú) is deliberately left, for the pun 年年有余 (niánnián yǒu yú) — "surplus every year."

Myth: slurping is a compliment

That's Japan. Western content transplanted it onto China and it stuck.

The honest picture: slurping is common and unremarkable in a casual noodle shop, and mildly bad manners at a formal table — Chinese-language etiquette writing treats mouth noise (吧唧嘴) as a negative. It is not a compliment to the chef and nobody is waiting for you to do it. If you're slurping to be polite, you've imported the wrong country's rule.

Half-myth: chopsticks upright in rice

The taboo is real — upright chopsticks resemble incense burned for the dead, and the association is universally recognised.

What's distorted is the framing. Western content presents this as the chopstick rule, with catastrophic stakes. The Chinese catering industry's own list ranks it as one of roughly eight taboos, alongside several you've probably never heard of and are breaking regularly:

  • Pointing with your chopsticks — your forefinger ends up aimed at people all meal
  • Tapping them on the bowl — a beggar's gesture
  • Digging around in a shared dish to find the good bits
  • Hovering indecisively over the dishes
  • Sucking or licking them
  • Crossing them — reads as negation or conflict

In practice you eat rice from a bowl you lift and rest your chopsticks on a rest or across the bowl, so the upright situation barely arises. Meanwhile you're almost certainly pointing with them.

Myth: tipping causes offence

Tipping is not expected in mainland China — restaurants, taxis, delivery, salons, none of it. But "it causes offence" is overcooked. Nobody's insulted; the concept just isn't part of the transaction.

Real exceptions: tour guides (roughly ¥50–200 a day is the genuine convention), and optional small amounts at five-star international hotels. Hong Kong and Macau are different — 10% service charges are commonly added and small tips are appreciated.

Note also that upscale mainland restaurants may add a 服务费 (service charge) of 10–15%. That's a charge, not a tip, and it isn't discretionary.

Myth: 小姐 means "Miss"

It used to. Older phrasebooks still teach it for addressing a waitress. In mainland China the word acquired a sex-work connotation from the 1990s onwards and you should not use it for staff. It retains its polite original sense in Singapore and Malaysia, and it's fine in mainland China attached to a surname in formal contexts (李小姐), but not as a way to call someone over.

Say 服务员 (fúwùyuán). Which brings us to the next thing.

How ordering actually works now

Calling out 服务员 across the room is normal and not rude. This surprises Westerners more than almost anything else on this list. Raising a hand works too. Hotpot and chain restaurants often have a call button on the table.

QR-code ordering (扫码点餐) is now the default in most sit-down restaurants — you scan, browse, order and pay from your phone, and the order goes straight to the kitchen. Many mid-range places have dropped paper menus entirely.

But the "China is fully QR-only" line is only half true, and there's an interesting reason. Chinese consumers pushed back hard on being forced to scan, hand over a phone number, or follow a marketing account just to see a menu — and regulators broadly sided with them. Consumer-protection regulations in force since July 2024 prohibit operators from forcing or covertly forcing consumers to hand over personal data unrelated to the transaction, and a national practice guide specifically covering QR-code ordering and personal data was published in September 2025. You are allowed to ask for a human.

If a restaurant's mini-program demands a Chinese mobile number you don't have, the workaround is simply to ask staff to order for you:

你好，我没有中国手机号，可以帮我点菜吗？
Nǐ hǎo, wǒ méiyǒu Zhōngguó shǒujī hào, kěyǐ bāng wǒ diǎn cài ma?
"Hi — I don't have a Chinese phone number. Could you help me order?"

Hotpot, specifically

It isn't always communal any more

Single-serving hotpot (小火锅, one person one pot) has become a major category in its own right — roughly ¥400 billion in 2025, about 48,000 outlets, some 10% of all hotpot restaurants, with big chains piling in. The "hotpot is inherently a group activity" framing is now partly out of date. Eating alone is a normal, well-served option.

The nine-grid pot is not nine flavours

A common misreading. The 九宫格 is one shared broth divided by metal partitions into heat zones, not nine different soups. Classically: the centre grid runs hottest (for tripe, goose intestine), the cross grids are medium (meatballs, softer items), the corners coolest (offal, duck blood). The origin is practical — dock workers in Chongqing partitioning a single pot between them.

Treat that heat map as idealised, though. The grids also just keep things organised and stop small items getting lost, and the real heat distribution depends on the burner, the pot, the broth level and the restaurant.

Raw and cooked, and why it matters

This isn't fussiness. Chinese food-safety advisories for hotpot are specific:

  • Use separate chopsticks and strainers for raw and cooked. 生熟分开 — this is the one that matters most.
  • Cook meat, offal, seafood and meatballs all the way through. Don't eat anything half-raw.
  • Keep the broth boiling, and when you top it up, bring it back to a boil before adding food.

Separately, 公筷 (gōngkuài) — communal serving chopsticks, used to move food from shared dishes to your bowl — became a widespread norm after 2020. Asking for a pair is unremarkable and slightly classy.

"Seven up, eight down" is about texture, not safety

七上八下 is the famous rule for cooking beef tripe (毛肚) — swish it up and down fifteen times or so. It happens to land near the roughly fifteen seconds in boiling broth that actually kills most pathogens.

But it's a texture mnemonic, not a safety guarantee. Swish too fast and the tripe isn't properly heated — Chinese science-communication coverage cites a documented food-poisoning outbreak from undercooked tripe. And it doesn't transfer to offal, meatballs, or anything thick.

We're deliberately not printing a cooking-time table. The best sources actively warn against them: timing changes with thickness, starting temperature, how full the pot is and how hard it's boiling. Watch the food, not the clock.

The sauce bar is regional

RegionDipWhy
Sichuan / Chongqing油碟 yóudié — sesame oil, minced garlic, sometimes scallion, coriander, vinegarThe oil cools food fast so you don't burn your mouth, and buffers the 麻辣 hit
Northern China麻酱 májiàng — sesame pasteNorthern lamb hotpot broth is nearly plain water with ginger and scallion, so the flavour has to come from the dip

Chongqing also has 干油碟, a dry chilli-powder dip, which increases the heat rather than tempering it. Know what you're reaching for.

Sauce-bar etiquette: don't pour unused dipping sauce back into the pot, don't fish other people's food out without asking, and don't top up the broth without checking with the table — it dilutes what everyone's built up.

Spice levels aren't standardised

不辣 → 微辣 → 中辣 → 特辣, plus 变态辣 at some chains. There have been several attempts at a real standard — a 2018 Chongqing local standard even converted Scoville units into spice degrees — but they're voluntary and largely ignored. One restaurant's mild is another's medium.

Useful order: 鸳鸯锅 (yuānyāng guō) — the split pot, half spicy 红汤, half clear 清汤. It's the diplomatic answer for a mixed table and nobody thinks less of you for it.

Receipt vs invoice — a small trap. 发票 (fāpiào) is the official tax invoice, issued through the tax system; the restaurant may ask you for a company name and tax number. If you just want proof of payment, the word is 小票 (xiǎopiào), the till receipt. Asking for a 发票 when you wanted a slip of paper creates confusion on both sides.

If you remember three things

  1. Order for the table, and finish what you order. The old "leave a little" advice is gone.
  2. Call the server out loud, point at what you want, and don't tip. All three are normal.
  3. At hotpot, separate raw and cooked, and cook things through. Everything else is style.

You know the rules. Now say the words.
