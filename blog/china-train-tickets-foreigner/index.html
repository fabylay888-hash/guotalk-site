How to Book China High-Speed Train Tickets as a Foreigner (2026)

Published August 6, 2026 · by the GuoTalk team

The short version

  • Register on the official 12306 English platform and complete passport verification weeks before you travel. You can't buy anything until it clears, and the manual review path can take days.
  • No Chinese phone number needed on the English platform — an email address is enough.
  • There is no paper ticket. Your passport is the ticket, and it must be the same physical passport you booked with.
  • Standard sale window is 15 days ahead, counting the day you buy. Release time is staggered by departure station, not a single national hour.
  • Foreign card declines on 12306 are common. Have a second payment route ready before you need it.

Booking direct on 12306 (the official way)

China Railway's own platform is 12306.cn/en, with a mobile app called Railway 12306. It's free of markup and it's what we'd use for anything outside peak season.

Be aware of what it is, though: China Railway describes the English platform as "a simplified version to facilitate foreign passport holders," and it is genuinely reduced. More on what's missing below.

Registration and passport verification

  1. Register in the app or on the website with your details and an email address. A foreign mobile number is fine; you don't need a +86 one.
  2. Click the activation link that arrives by email. If it doesn't show up, wait about half an hour before trying again, and try switching between the website and the app — both have been reported flaky.
  3. Complete real-name verification. Since November 2023 foreigners can do this online: either the system matches your name, nationality and document number automatically, or you upload a photo of your passport information page for manual review. Presenting the passport at a station window is still available as a fallback.
Do this early — it's the single biggest planning mistake. You cannot buy a ticket or add passengers until verification clears. The automatic match can be near-instant; the manual photo-review path has been reported as taking three to five working days. Sources genuinely disagree on the timing, which is exactly why you should allow several days and do it before you leave home.

Paying — the real friction point

The English platform officially accepts international cards and China UnionPay, and Visa, Mastercard, JCB and Diners Club are all reported working. Alipay and WeChat Pay work too if you've linked a card.

The honest position, though, is that foreign card declines are common enough to plan around. There are first-hand reports of cards that worked fine from abroad failing repeatedly once the traveller was inside China. Repeated failures can also flag your account for manual review, which makes things worse.

So: set up a second route before you need it. Having Alipay or WeChat Pay linked to a foreign card (see our payments guide) is the most useful backup, and a third-party booking site is the third.

What the English platform can't do

  • No waitlist (候补). This is the big one. When tickets sell out, Chinese travellers submit a standby order, and released tickets go to waitlist holders first — you can list up to 60 date-and-train combinations. The English platform deliberately excludes this feature. At Spring Festival or Golden Week, that's the strongest argument for using an agency.
  • No seat selection. Seats are auto-assigned.
  • Three order cancellations per day, maximum. Exceed it and you're blocked from buying for the rest of the day.
  • Service hours: ticket sales and changes run 05:00–01:00. Queries and refunds are broader.

Third-party booking sites

Trip.com is the best-known; Klook and a few rail specialists also sell China rail. Expect roughly US$3–5 per ticket in fees — figures vary by route and promotion, and the booking fee itself is non-refundable. Trip.com also adds its own change fee on top of the railway's penalties, so if there's any chance you'll cancel, booking direct is cheaper.

The genuine advantage is timing. Agencies accept reservation requests months ahead and attempt the purchase the instant the official window opens. If you're travelling at peak and can't use the Chinese-only waitlist, that automation is worth paying for.

One thing to be clear-eyed about: an agency cannot conjure inventory that doesn't exist. They queue at the same moment everyone else does. What you're buying is persistence and English support, not privileged access.

Your passport is the ticket

There is no paper artefact in a normal domestic journey any more. E-tickets went nationwide across high-speed rail in April 2020 and conventional rail that June. Paper reimbursement vouchers were discontinued from 1 October 2025 — the receipt is now an electronic invoice.

(You'll see articles claiming electronic tickets launched in November 2024. That's the electronic invoice date, and it gets repeated a lot.)

At the gate: at most major stations the automatic gates have a passport reader — place the photo page face-down on the scanner. But this isn't universal and it doesn't always read cleanly, especially on older or worn passports. Every station also has a staffed lane, signed 人工通道 (réngōng tōngdào), usually at the far left or far right end of the gate array. If the automatic gate rejects you, that's where you go. It takes seconds and it's completely routine — just budget a couple of extra minutes rather than assuming.

Carry the original passport you booked with. Not a copy, not a photo, not a different passport you happen to also hold. You'll show it at the station entrance, at the gate, and possibly on board.

Timing: when tickets go on sale, and how fast they go

The standard window is 15 days, inclusive of the day you buy — so effectively fourteen days ahead.

There is no single national release time. Release is staggered by departure station, spread through the day. The same train can be released at noon from one station and 16:00 from another. In the Chinese 12306 app you can look this up under 我的 → 出行向导 → 起售时间; whether the English app exposes the same tool, we can't confirm. If you're chasing a specific peak ticket, it's worth finding out your station's time rather than setting an 08:00 alarm on faith.

How bad does peak get?

Bad. When Spring Festival eve tickets were released in early 2026, popular routes out of Beijing — to Shijiazhuang, Zhengzhou, Wuhan and Xi'an — sold out essentially on release, within seconds. Off-peak, booking three to four days ahead is usually comfortable; same-day availability is unreliable.

New: a 60-day advance reservation pilot

From 20 July 2026, China Railway began piloting a much longer reservation window on the Beijing–Shanghai corridor — over 200 trains across the high-speed and conventional lines. You submit a request 60 to 17 days out; the system fulfils requests 20–16 days before departure and notifies you, and payment is due within 23 hours. The full window becomes standard from around 30 August 2026, applying to departures from mid-September onward.

We can't confirm whether this is available on the English platform or to foreign passport holders. Given the English build already excludes the waitlist, there's a real chance it's Chinese-only for now. Worth checking when you book; don't plan around it yet.

On Beijing–Shanghai (1,318 km, roughly 4h15m to 5h30m, something like 40–50 G-trains a day), as observed in August 2026: second class runs roughly ¥550–680, first class roughly ¥930–1,110, business class roughly ¥1,750–2,460.

Treat every price as indicative. The line has used dynamic pricing since 2020 — fares move with time of day, day of week, season and how many stops the train makes. In May 2026 the published fare ceiling was raised 20% (second class max ¥820) while discounts were deepened at the same time, so the prices actually charged barely moved. That leaves real headroom above today's fares. Check the live price when you book.

Overnight sleepers are worth a thought on long routes: a soft sleeper can cost less than the day train and saves you a hotel night.

At the station

Arrive 45–60 minutes early at a normal station. Give yourself 75–90 minutes at the mega-stations — Beijing South, Shanghai Hongqiao, Guangzhou South — and during holidays. Hong Kong West Kowloon involves immigration, so allow at least 45 minutes there, more at peak.

The sequence:

  1. Entrance ID check. Passport out; staff confirm you have a valid e-ticket for that day.
  2. Security. Airport-style: bags through the X-ray, metal detector, hand-wand.
  3. Find your waiting room. The big departure board maps your train number to a numbered waiting hall. This signage is often Chinese-only and it's genuinely easy to sit in the wrong hall for forty minutes.
  4. Gate (检票口). Opens roughly 15 minutes before departure and closes 5 minutes before. That cut-off is enforced.
  5. Platform. Carriage numbers are marked on the platform floor and pillars.

Stations are big and busy. Keep your bag close — pickpocketing at major stations is a real and frequently-flagged problem.

The power bank rule is the one that bites. If the capacity marking has rubbed off yours, security can and does confiscate it. It's reportedly the most common way travellers lose gear at Chinese stations. Check yours before you pack.

Baggage allowance is 20 kg for adults, with dimensions summing to 130 cm or less on high-speed trains. Folding wheelchairs and collapsible buggies travel free and don't count.

Travelling with children

Under 6 travel free, one child per adult; 6–14 are discounted; 14 and over pay full fare. Each child needs their own valid ID — your passport doesn't cover them — and a free-travelling under-6 must be declared at the time of booking.

The mistakes that actually cost people trains

  1. Leaving 12306 verification until you need a ticket. Do it weeks ahead.
  2. No backup payment method. Foreign cards fail on 12306 more than they should.
  3. Booking the wrong station. Chinese cities routinely have several high-speed stations, sometimes an hour apart. Search by station name, not city name.
  4. Misjudging the gate cut-off. Five minutes before departure, and the station is a kilometre long.
  5. Name mismatch between passport and booking. 12306 has specific rules for entering foreign names and numbers; it isn't free text. Match your passport exactly.
  6. Not knowing release is staggered by station. An 08:00 alarm for a station that releases at 16:00 is a wasted morning.
  7. Expecting to buy peak tickets on the day. At Spring Festival, popular routes are gone in seconds.
