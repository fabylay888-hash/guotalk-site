How to Order Food Delivery in China Without Speaking Chinese

Published August 6, 2026 · by the GuoTalk team

The short version

  • Ele.me no longer exists as a brand. It was renamed 淘宝闪购 (Taobao Shangou) in December 2025. Any guide telling you to download Ele.me is out of date.
  • Meituan (美团) is the easier one for tourists — it accepts Visa and Mastercard directly. Taobao Shangou routes payment through WeChat Pay.
  • Never type your hotel name in English into the address field. Get the address in Chinese characters and paste it.
  • Assume you're going down to the lobby. Delivery to your room is a bonus, not the default.
  • The single most-typed note in Chinese food delivery is 不要香菜 — "no coriander." You may need it.

The apps, as they actually are in 2026

Two platforms matter, plus one making a lot of noise:

  • Meituan (美团) — the market leader, running around 65 million food-delivery orders a day in early 2026.
  • Taobao Shangou (淘宝闪购) — Alibaba's platform, around 50 million a day. This is Ele.me: same app, same app-store listing, same operating company. Alibaba retired the Ele.me brand in December 2025.
  • JD (京东外卖) — entered in early 2025 and growing fast, but still much smaller. Not where a visitor should start.

Douyin has a large local-services business, but it's mostly group-buy vouchers rather than restaurant delivery. Not a practical option for a tourist.

Do not go looking for Sherpa's. The long-running English-language delivery service in Shanghai and Beijing closed in December 2024. Several travel guides published since still recommend it. If a China guide is recommending Sherpa's, stop reading it — it hasn't been checked in two years.

Getting in: accounts and payment

Payment (this part is clear)

Meituan accepts foreign cards directly — Visa and Mastercard, plus Discover and Diners Club. Amex and JCB work by routing through WeChat Pay. Taobao Shangou does not take direct foreign-card binding; you pay via WeChat Pay.

That difference alone is why we'd point a card-carrying tourist at Meituan first.

If you're going through Alipay or WeChat Pay, the fee rule is worth knowing: transactions of ¥200 or less are free; above ¥200 there's a 3% charge. Almost every food-delivery order is under ¥200, so most visitors pay no platform surcharge at all. (Your own bank's foreign-transaction fee still applies.) Full setup walkthrough in our payments guide.

Accounts (this part is messier)

Whether you can register Meituan with a foreign phone number is genuinely contested — we found four recent guides giving three incompatible answers, all within six months of each other. So here's the advice that holds regardless of who's right:

  1. Log in with your WeChat or Alipay account rather than registering from scratch. This sidesteps the SMS question entirely and it's the route every guide converges on.
  2. Expect the delivery address's contact-number field to be stricter than the login. That field is the one most likely to demand a mainland +86 number, and it's a separate problem from signing in. Most articles miss this distinction.
  3. A local SIM removes every version of this problem. It's a five-minute purchase at any major airport arrivals hall — see our guide to getting online in China.

Will the app be in English?

Sort of, and it won't help as much as you'd hope. Meituan's App Store listing declares English support, but the part you actually need to read — restaurant names, dish names, menu descriptions — is text merchants typed in Chinese. No app localisation translates that.

This is the real argument for the WeChat mini-program route. Since October 2024 WeChat has had a built-in mini-program translation feature covering 18 languages including English: set your target language under Me → Settings → General → Translation, then use the button in the mini-program's top-right corner. That translation layer sits over the whole ordering flow, merchant-entered text included. The standalone Meituan app has nothing equivalent.

Alipay is also useful here, though not for the reason most guides claim. Its app runs in 16 languages and has a translation tool on the home page that handles text, audio and photos — so it will translate a photographed menu. What Alipay does not appear to have is a dedicated foreigner-friendly delivery product: food delivery is conspicuously absent from every official Alipay inbound-tourist feature announcement we could find. Alipay solves payment and translation. It doesn't solve ordering.

The address problem

This is where most first attempts fail. Chinese address matching runs on Chinese strings. Type "Hilton Shanghai" into the address field and the system will not find it.

What to do instead:

  1. Get the address in Chinese characters from your booking confirmation — Trip.com, Booking.com and Agoda all carry it — or ask the front desk. Better still, ask them to type it into your phone, so you have copy-pasteable text rather than a photo.
  2. Paste it in. Don't retype it.
  3. Or pin the location on the map inside the app and let it auto-fill the Chinese string.
  4. Put your room number in its own field (门牌号, door number), not buried in the address line.

Chinese addresses run largest-unit-first, the inverse of the Western order:

上海市黄浦区南京东路123号1205室
Shànghǎi shì / Huángpǔ qū / Nánjīng dōng lù / 123 hào / 1205 shì
Shanghai City / Huangpu District / Nanjing East Road / No. 123 / Room 1205

If you want to sanity-check that a Chinese address string is really your hotel, photograph it and run it through Alipay's image translation.

What happens when it arrives

Assume the lobby

Chinese hotels handle deliveries in one of four ways: the rider comes up (often discouraged for security reasons), staff relay it up, a delivery robot takes it, or — most commonly — it waits at the front desk or in a locker and you come down. Hotel trade press is blunt about this: when deliveries pile up, guests are asked to collect. Plan for that and you'll never be annoyed by it.

The phone call

The rider will probably call. Two things you should know before that happens.

First, the number is a masked relay number, not the rider's real one — and yours is masked too. Meituan has run a privacy-number system since 2017; the virtual number expires the moment the order completes. So you can't call them back later, and you can't reliably text it.

Second, don't just ignore it. Plenty of travel blogs tell you to let it ring — "the driver will find you." That advice runs against how the system actually works. When a rider can't reach you, the documented procedure is to escalate to the platform, which then calls you itself; if that also fails, the rider is released from responsibility and the food either goes back to the restaurant or waits for a second attempt. Ignoring the call is the path to no dinner.

Better: pre-empt it in the notes field, then answer and say one sentence.

The words that actually matter

You need eight phrases, not eighty. Paste these straight into the notes box (备注 bèizhù).

ChinesePinyinWhat it does
不要香菜bú yào xiāngcàiNo coriander / cilantro
微辣wēi làMildly spicy
不辣bú làNot spicy at all

无需餐具wúxū cānjùNo cutlery needed
请放在前台，谢谢qǐng fàng zài qiántái, xièxiePlease leave it at the front desk, thank you
我不会说中文wǒ bú huì shuō zhōngwénI don't speak Chinese

A pronunciation note, because we're a Mandarin app and this matters. Pinyin is usually written in the dictionary form, but two characters change tone in speech. 不 becomes bú before a fourth-tone syllable — so 不要 is said bú yào, not bù yào. And 一 becomes yí before a fourth tone — so 一份 is said yí fèn. We've written the spoken forms above, because that's what you'd actually say.

Spice: the scale is not a scale

微辣 (wēi là, mild) → 中辣 (zhōng là, medium) → 特辣 (tè là, extra) → 变态辣 (biàntài là, literally "freak spicy," a novelty tier at some chains — don't).

But none of this is standardised. Official scales exist and restaurants largely ignore them; chains calibrate tightly, small independent kitchens go by feel, and everyone calibrates to their local customers. 微辣 in Chengdu or Chongqing is not 微辣 in Shanghai. Order one level below what you think you want.

One more distinction worth having: 辣 (là) is chilli burn. 麻 (má) is the numbing tingle of Sichuan pepper. 麻辣 (málà) is both at once, and it's a different experience from "spicy."

Coriander, and why it has its own phrase

香菜 (xiāngcài, literally "fragrant vegetable") is coriander. In an analysis of Chinese delivery order notes, it was the single most-rejected ingredient — and, delightfully, also the second most-requested, because the people who like it really like it. Ninety percent of the flavour-related notes people write concern spice; the rest is largely this.

Use 香菜, not 芫荽. Both are real words; only one is universally understood.

Allergies — please read this bit

The sentence frame is 我对…过敏 (wǒ duì … guòmǐn, "I'm allergic to…"): 花生 huāshēng peanut, 海鲜 hǎixiān seafood, 鸡蛋 jīdàn egg, 牛奶 niúnǎi milk.

But a free-text note in a delivery app is not an allergy-management system. It goes to a busy kitchen, it may not be read, and nothing verifies it. If you have a serious allergy, do not rely on typing 我对花生过敏 into a remarks box. Eat somewhere you can talk to a person.

Five mistakes to skip

  1. Following advice written before December 2025. Ele.me is renamed; Sherpa's is gone.
  2. Typing the hotel name in English. The matcher needs Chinese characters.
  3. Expecting room service, then ignoring the call. Lobby is the norm, and an unreachable customer is how food goes back to the restaurant.
  4. Trusting the spice label. There's no enforced standard. Go one level down.
  5. Being surprised by packaging fees. 打包费 is usually charged per item, even when everything arrives in one bag, and it often only appears at checkout. A single order can easily carry ¥10 of it.

Bonus: ticking "no cutlery" doesn't reliably mean no cutlery. Take it as a request, not a setting.

Copy-paste trap. Some English guides print these phrases in traditional characters — 不要打電話 rather than 不要打电话. Mainland platforms want simplified. Everything on this page is simplified.
