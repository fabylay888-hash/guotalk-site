How to Pay in China as a Tourist (2026): Alipay & WeChat Pay, Step by Step

Updated July 24, 2026 · by the GuoTalk team

The short version

  • China runs on phone QR payments. Your physical Visa/Mastercard will be refused at most shops and restaurants.
  • Set up Alipay (primary) and WeChat Pay (backup) before you fly, and link your normal home card to each.
  • Payments of ¥200 or less are fee-free; above ¥200 there's a 3% charge on the whole amount.
  • Carry ¥300–500 in cash as a safety net — you'll rarely need it, but sometimes you will.

The one thing to understand before you land

China skipped credit cards almost entirely and went straight from cash to phones. Locals pay for everything — a ¥6 bottle of tea, a ¥600 dinner, a taxi, a train ticket — by scanning a QR code with Alipay (支付宝) or WeChat Pay (微信支付). Card terminals that accept foreign Visa or Mastercard exist mainly in international hotels and airport shops, and nowhere else you'll actually eat.

The good news: since 2024, both apps let foreign visitors link an international card directly — no Chinese bank account, no Chinese phone number. Ten minutes of setup at home saves you a genuinely stressful first day.

Step 1: Set up Alipay (do this one first)

Alipay is the easier of the two for visitors and the one we'd rely on day-to-day.

  1. Download Alipay from the App Store or Google Play while still in your home country.
  2. Register with your home phone number. Foreign numbers work — pick your country code at sign-up. Keep that SIM reachable (roaming or eSIM) for verification texts.
  3. Link your card: tap Me → Bank Cards → + and enter your card number. Visa, Mastercard, American Express, JCB, Diners Club and international UnionPay cards are all supported.
  4. Verify your identity when prompted. At some point — often on your first payment or when linking the card — the app asks for a passport photo and a quick selfie. This is normal and required by Chinese regulation; approval is usually minutes.
  5. Do a test. You can't fully test payments until you're in China, but make sure the card shows as linked with no warning message before you fly.
Heads-up on debit cards: some banks decline Alipay/WeChat charges as "foreign online transactions." A travel-friendly credit card tends to link more reliably than a small local bank's debit card. Link two different cards if you can — redundancy costs nothing.

Step 2: Set up WeChat Pay (your backup)

A minority of small vendors — street stalls, some mom-and-pop shops — take only WeChat. That's why you want both.

  1. Download WeChat and register with your home number.
  2. Find the wallet: Me → Services → Wallet → Bank Cards → Add a Card. (If "Services" is hidden: Settings → General → Manage Discover Page.)
  3. Add your Visa/Mastercard and complete the same passport verification when asked.

Practical reading of that table: everyday spending — meals, metro, snacks, taxis — mostly sits under ¥200 per transaction, so most tourists pay almost nothing in fees. For bigger bills, remember your home bank may also add its own foreign-transaction fee on top.

What you can do inside the apps (more than paying)

  • Didi (ride-hailing): runs as a mini-program inside both Alipay and WeChat — no separate app, no Chinese account needed. This is how you'll take taxis. Full walkthrough: Didi without a Chinese phone number.
  • Metro: in most big cities, Alipay's Transport section gives you a QR code you scan at the gates — no ticket machine queues.
  • Trains: book high-speed rail through the apps' train mini-programs or the official 12306 app/site with your passport number.
  • Food delivery, shared bikes, museum bookings: all live as mini-programs too. Alipay has an English mode that makes them navigable.

Cash: still worth carrying (a little)

Merchants in China are legally required to accept cash, and it remains the fallback when a card won't link or your phone dies. ¥300–500 covers you. Get it from your home bank or a Chinese ATM (major-bank ATMs — BOC, ICBC — take foreign cards). Expect small vendors to grumble about change for a ¥100 note.

Common problems and quick fixes

  • Card declined at linking: try your other card, or the same card in the other app — acceptance genuinely differs between Alipay and WeChat. Calling your bank to pre-authorize "China / online" transactions often fixes it.
  • Verification loop or rejected passport photo: use good lighting, all four corners of the photo page visible, no glare. Retry once; it usually passes the second time.
  • Payment fails at the counter: switch apps and try again — takes ten seconds and works surprisingly often.
  • No, you don't need a VPN for these apps. Alipay, WeChat and Didi all work on Chinese networks out of the box. (You'll want a VPN or roaming eSIM for Google/Instagram — different problem, different guide.)
