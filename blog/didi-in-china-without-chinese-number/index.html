Didi in China Without a Chinese Phone Number (2026)

Updated July 31, 2026 · by the GuoTalk team

The short version

  • You don't need a Chinese SIM or a Chinese bank account. Didi accepts international phone numbers and foreign cards.
  • Set it up at home, before you fly — verification can take up to 24 hours and you don't want to discover that at the airport.
  • The eSIM trap: a data-only travel eSIM can't receive the SMS verification code. Keep a number that can receive texts.
  • If your card is refused, the fallback is the Didi mini-program inside Alipay or WeChat — it charges your wallet instead.
  • At airports and stations, walk to the marked ride-hail zone (网约车) before you book.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Uber doesn't operate in China. It sold its China business to Didi in 2016, so if you open Uber in Shanghai, nothing happens. Didi (滴滴出行) runs ride-hailing in over 400 Chinese cities, and outside of the metro, it is how you get around.

The old advice — "you need a Chinese phone number and a Chinese bank card" — is out of date. Since the 2024 push to make China easier for foreign visitors, Didi supports international registration and foreign cards directly. But the setup has a few specific traps that will absolutely catch you if nobody warns you, and most guides skip them.

Step 1: Install and register before you leave home

Do this on your home Wi-Fi, days before your flight. Not at the airport.

  1. Install Didi from your normal App Store or Google Play. Search "DiDi" — you want the international-facing rider app with an English interface, not a Chinese-language-only listing.
  2. Register with your home mobile number. Select your country code, enter your number, and wait for the SMS verification code. Foreign numbers are supported.
  3. Complete identity verification if prompted. Some travellers are asked for a passport photo page and a selfie; others aren't. Reports vary, so have your passport to hand. When it is required, approval can take anywhere from a few minutes to 24 hours — which is the single best reason to do this a week early.
  4. Add a payment method (next section).
  5. Test a destination search while still at home. You won't be able to complete a ride, but you'll find out now if something is broken.
⚠️ The eSIM trap — this is the one that catches people. Plenty of travellers buy a data-only China eSIM and turn off their home SIM to dodge roaming charges. A data-only eSIM has no voice or SMS service, so it cannot receive the verification code — not for Didi, and not for the re-verification prompts you may hit later. Either keep your home number reachable for texts, or buy an eSIM that includes a real number with SMS.

Step 2: Payment — and what to do when your card is refused

Didi accepts international Visa, Mastercard, JCB and Diners Club cards, plus Alipay, WeChat Pay and Apple Pay. Charges land on your statement in your home currency.

Card rejections are common enough that you should plan for one rather than be surprised by it. Chinese platforms trip a lot of banks' fraud rules, and small local-bank debit cards fare worst.

The mini-program route (a genuinely good plan B)

If you've already set up Alipay or WeChat Pay with a foreign card — and you should have, see our payments guide — you can skip the standalone Didi app entirely. Open Alipay or WeChat, search for Didi in the mini-programs section, and book from there.

The trade-off: the mini-program uses only that wallet as its payment method, and its menus differ from the main app. As a primary it's clunkier. As a backup at 11pm when your card won't work, it's the difference between a ride and a very long walk.

Step 3: Actually taking the ride

  • At airports and train stations, go to the ride-hail zone first. Look for signs marked 网约车 (wǎngyuēchē — "online-booked car"). These are specific numbered bays, often in a parking structure, and they are not where taxis queue. Booking before you get there means your driver arrives, can't find you, and cancels.
  • Your driver almost certainly speaks no English. The app auto-translates in-app messages in both directions, and this works well enough for "I'm at exit B" or "I see you". Use it rather than trying to talk.
  • Have your destination in Chinese characters. Ask your hotel to write the address down, or screenshot it. Pinyin is useless to a driver — it isn't how Chinese is read.
  • Don't tip. Tipping isn't customary in China and the app has no tip button. Nobody expects it and nobody is offended.
  • Cancellation is free within about three minutes, then a small fee (roughly ¥3–5) applies.
  • Cash only works on the traditional-taxi option, and even then it's unreliable. Treat online payment as the only real method.
Surge pricing: if the estimate looks high, switch to the metered taxi option inside Didi, or wait ten to fifteen minutes. Chinese surge pricing tends to settle faster than in Western cities.

The alternative: Amap

Amap (高德地图, Gāodé Dìtú) is China's dominant maps app and also aggregates ride-hailing across multiple providers, including Didi. It now has an English mode. Two reasons to have it installed alongside Didi:

  • It's your Google Maps replacement — Google Maps is effectively unusable in China.
  • Its ride aggregation sometimes surfaces a cheaper or faster car than booking through Didi alone.

For most trips Didi is simpler. But when Didi says twenty minutes at 2× price, it's worth checking.

Do you need a VPN for any of this?

No. Didi, Amap, Alipay and WeChat all work perfectly on Chinese networks. A VPN is for Google, Instagram, WhatsApp and similar — a separate problem, and one to sort out before you land, since VPN sites are hard to reach from inside China.

Quick pre-flight checklist

  1. Didi installed, registered with your home number ✅
  2. Identity verification cleared (if you were asked) ✅
  3. Card linked, with a second card as backup ✅
  4. Alipay set up as the fallback payment route ✅
  5. A phone number that can receive SMS while you're in China ✅
  6. Amap installed ✅
  7. Hotel address saved in Chinese characters ✅
